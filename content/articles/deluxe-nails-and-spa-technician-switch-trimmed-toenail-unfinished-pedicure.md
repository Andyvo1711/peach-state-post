Regular clients are the backbone of any salon, and they tend to notice even small changes in how they are treated. A recent review of Deluxe Nails & Spa comes from exactly this kind of guest. She introduced herself with the words "I am a loyal customer" and explained that she visits every two weeks. Her account of one appointment, which did not go as she hoped, offers useful lessons on handoffs between technicians, how to handle a service dispute, and how to protect a long-term relationship.

## A Handoff in the Middle of the Service

According to the customer, one technician began her pedicure and was then replaced by another partway through. Changes like this happen in busy salons for good reasons, but from the client's chair they can feel abrupt, especially for someone who has a trusted routine. The reviewer says the second technician trimmed the tip off one of her toenails, which she had paid for just two weeks earlier, and that this left her nail shapes uneven.

A simple handoff routine can help. The first technician can introduce the next one, describe what has already been done, and confirm the client's usual preferences, such as length, shape, and any enhancements already on the nails. Taking a moment to ask before altering a nail the client has recently invested in prevents most surprises.

## An Upsell Conversation That Missed the Mark

The customer reports that the technician suggested a new acrylic toenail to correct the shape. Because she already had one in place when she arrived, she declined, and she felt there had been no reason to change it. In her words, "there was nothing wrong with it," and she asked for the nail to be replaced at no charge.

Offering a repair option is a helpful instinct, but timing and tone matter. When a change to a client's nail has caused the issue, the first step is to acknowledge it and offer a solution, not to present an additional paid service. The reviewer also described feeling that the technician did not seem to understand what she was saying and that the conversation became tense, with the technician asking the team to start on another client. Even when communication is difficult, staying calm, listening to the full request, and inviting a manager to join can steer a conversation back to a solution.

## Finishing the Service and Repairing the Relationship

The customer says she paid for her nails but felt that her pedicure was left incomplete and the trimmed toenail was never replaced. She also shared that she was not fully pleased with the manicure that was completed during the visit. Her closing thoughts were clear: "I will not be back."

An unfinished service is one of the most fixable problems in the industry, as long as it is addressed before the client leaves. A manager or senior technician can step in, apologize, complete the pedicure, repair or replace the affected nail at the salon's cost, and offer a small gesture of thanks to a guest who has visited every two weeks. A follow-up message a day or two later shows that the salon values the relationship.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers three lessons for nail teams. First, plan handoffs carefully by introducing the new technician, sharing what has been done, and asking before changing any nail the client recently paid for. Second, when a mistake affects a guest's nails, lead with acknowledgment and a no-cost solution, and keep upsell suggestions for a separate, calm moment. Third, bring in a manager early when a conversation becomes tense, and make sure every paid service is completed before the guest leaves.

Loyal clients rarely leave over a single error. They leave when the response to that error does not show that their loyalty matters.
