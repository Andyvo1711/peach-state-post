Some of the most useful feedback a salon receives is about what happens after the client has left. A recent review of Royal Nail & Spa describes a pedicure that looked good in the chair but did not hold up by the end of the same day. The customer's account is brief, yet it points to a few simple habits that can make the difference between a one-time visit and a regular client.

## A Friendly Visit That Started Well

The customer shared that her toes looked good when she left, and she was complimentary about the team, noting that "the staff is friendly." That is worth recognizing. A warm welcome and a pleasant atmosphere are the foundation of a good salon experience, and they are qualities not every business can offer.

It also shows that clients can feel positive about people and still be disappointed by a result. Friendliness opens the door, but a lasting finish is what encourages a return visit.

## Polish That Was Not Fully Dry

Later that night, the reviewer noticed that the polish on her left big toe had not dried completely and had started to peel. For a pedicure completed that same morning, this was frustrating, and it left her unwilling to book again.

Toenail polish is particularly sensitive to timing. The big toe has the largest nail, which means thicker coats take longer to dry, and it is also the area most likely to press against a shoe or a sheet. Several habits can help. Applying thin, even coats allows each layer to set properly, and a quick-dry top coat or drying drops can speed up the process. Making sure the nail surface is clean and free of oils before the base coat, and capping the free edge, improves adhesion. For gel polish, full curing under the lamp is essential, and the technician can check each toe for tackiness before the client stands up.

## Checking Before the Client Leaves

A short final check can catch most problems. Before the guest walks out, the technician can lightly look over each toe, especially the big toes, for smudges, dents, or soft spots. Offering a few extra minutes under a fan or lamp, and suggesting open-toe footwear for the first hours, helps protect the finish. Clients appreciate being told how long to wait before putting on socks or closed shoes, and that guidance costs nothing.

It is also helpful to let guests know they can come back if something goes wrong. A simple line such as "If anything smudges or lifts in the next few days, please call us and we will fix it" shows confidence in the work and gives a client a path to a solution, instead of a reason to write a review.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers three lessons for nail teams. First, protect the strengths of the visit, such as friendly service, and pair them with a finish that lasts. Second, apply polish in thin layers, allow adequate drying or curing time, and give extra attention to the big toes, where smudges happen most. Third, finish every pedicure with a quick check and clear aftercare advice, along with an open invitation to return for a touch-up if needed.

A pedicure is judged twice, once in the salon and again that evening at home. Teams that plan for the second moment tend to earn the loyalty that friendly service alone cannot secure.
