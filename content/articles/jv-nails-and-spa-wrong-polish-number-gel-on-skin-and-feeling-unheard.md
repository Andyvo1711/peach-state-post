Most appointments start with a simple exchange: the client names a color and the technician finds it. A customer of **JV Nails and Spa** describes how a small slip at that very first step set the tone for the whole visit. By her account, she left feeling "uncomfortable, unheard, and unhappy with the final result," and she does not plan to return.

Her story is a useful lesson in how a salon team handles mistakes, including the ones that come from the client.

## A Polite Correction That Felt Like an Inconvenience

The customer says she accidentally gave the wrong polish number at the start. To her credit, she spoke up before any painting began and asked to switch to the correct color. She felt the reaction made the request seem like an inconvenience.

Wrong numbers are common, especially with large color walls. The best response is a warm "No problem, let's get you the right one." That one sentence tells a client that speaking up is welcome. When the new bottle was brought out, she says it still did not look like the bright red she wanted, but after the earlier moment she felt awkward raising it again. A quick check at this stage, holding the bottle next to a photo or the original swatch and asking, "Is this the shade you had in mind?", would have solved the problem before it began.

## Gel on the Skin

Once painting began, the customer noticed the gel polish flooding into her cuticles and onto the skin around several nails. She saw signs that someone tried to wipe it away, but she felt it had already spread too far. She also knew gel on the skin tends to lift and peel quickly, which affects how long the manicure lasts.

Clean lines around the cuticle are one of the first things clients judge. Technicians can keep control by using thin coats, leaving a small gap at the cuticle, and cleaning any flooding before curing rather than after. Clients who are hesitant to speak up, as this one describes herself, rely on the technician to notice and fix these details without being asked.

## Fixing the Problem Together

When she eventually found the courage to ask for the polish to be removed, even if it meant leaving with bare nails, she felt there was pushback. Instead of starting over, she says the sides of her nails were drilled down to "clean it up," something she had never experienced. After more back and forth, the original polish was only partly removed, and several extra layers of another color were applied on top. She can still see the original color around the edges of many nails.

A calm conversation can change this outcome. Explain the options available, such as a full soak-off, a fresh start, or a color change, and let the client decide. Say what each choice involves, and make sure she is comfortable before any tool touches her nails.

## Editorial Perspective

This review reminds us that a client's comfort is part of the service, not an extra.

First, welcome color corrections warmly and confirm the shade against a photo before painting. Second, watch for flooding at the cuticle and fix it early, since it affects both appearance and durability. Third, when a client asks to start over, listen, offer clear options, and explain each step in plain words.

The customer wrote that she understands mistakes happen. That generosity is the opening a salon needs: a sincere acknowledgment and a clean fix can turn a disappointing visit into a loyal return.
