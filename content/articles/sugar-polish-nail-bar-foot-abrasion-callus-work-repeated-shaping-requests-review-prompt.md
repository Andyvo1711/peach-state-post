A manicure and pedicure before a long vacation is a treat many clients look forward to. A recent review of Sugar Polish Nail Bar on Prince Ave comes from a customer who booked exactly that, and who left feeling the visit fell short. Her account covers skin safety during callus work, communication during the manicure, and the way the salon asked for feedback. Together they offer useful lessons for nail teams.

## Callus Work and Skin Safety

The customer says her preferred technician was off that day, so she was assigned to another technician who, in her view, had done an average job on a previous visit. During the pedicure, she reports that the back of her foot felt as if it had been cut while her callus was being smoothed with a tool that "looked like a grater you'd use in the kitchen." It was only at home that she saw a sizeable patch of skin had been removed from her heel. She noted that the technician never said anything, and she was unsure whether it had been noticed. She applied antibiotic ointment, particularly since she was leaving on a long trip a few days later.

Callus care calls for a light touch and constant attention. Many state boards restrict blade-style callus tools, so teams should know their local rules and use approved, well-maintained files and rasps. Softening the skin first, keeping pressure gentle, and checking the heel frequently help prevent over-removal. If any skin is rubbed raw or broken, the best practice is to stop, tell the client right away, apply proper first aid, and offer clear aftercare guidance. Being open in that moment is far better for trust than a client discovering the issue later.

## Preferences, Matching, and Atmosphere

The customer described the manicure as "okay, not great." She said she had to keep explaining how she wanted her nails shaped and had to remind the technician that she prefers a high-gloss buff, even while the buffing was underway. She also felt that the team seemed to be going through the motions, without much warmth.

A few simple habits can help. A short intake at the start, asking about shape, length, and finish, takes less than a minute and can be noted in the client's profile for next time. When a preferred technician is unavailable, the front desk can offer to reschedule or let the client choose among those on duty, and mention her preferences to whoever takes the appointment. A friendly greeting and a calm, engaged manner also go a long way toward making the visit feel special. The reviewer added that she overheard another guest voicing discomfort during her pedicure, a reminder to check in with every client regularly about pressure and comfort.

## Feedback Requests That Feel Fair

Finally, the customer shared her perception of how the salon requests reviews. She said she received a text asking about her satisfaction, with a five-star option that led to a public review page, while a lower rating led to a message to the salon asking how it could improve. In her experience, she was only able to post her review after first selecting five stars.

Asking unhappy clients privately how to improve is a good idea, and many guests appreciate it. At the same time, review platforms generally encourage businesses to invite feedback from all customers in the same way, and clients can feel uneasy if the path to a public review seems to depend on their rating. A simple approach is to send the same link to everyone, and to follow up personally with anyone who shares a concern.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers three lessons for nail teams. First, treat callus and heel work with great care, use only approved tools, and tell the client immediately if any skin is affected, with first aid and aftercare. Second, collect preferences at the start of each visit, record them for future appointments, and keep the atmosphere warm and engaged. Third, ask for feedback in an open, consistent way, so that every client's voice is welcome and any concern can be addressed in person.

Clients tend to forgive a rough moment when they feel the team is honest and attentive. What they remember is how the salon responded.
