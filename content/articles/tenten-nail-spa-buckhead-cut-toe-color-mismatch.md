A longtime customer of TenTen Nail Spa Buckhead described her most recent visit as likely her last, after a pattern of uneven service quality culminated in a pedicure that left her toe injured. She noted that across several visits, her SNS manicures had ranged from acceptable to results that needed to be redone entirely, but it was a cuticle trim gone wrong during her latest pedicure that she said crossed a line she was not willing to overlook. The case is worth examining both for what it says about consistency across visits and for the specific safety concern raised during the pedicure itself.

## A Track Record of Inconsistent Results

According to the client, her experience at TenTen had been uneven over multiple visits, with some SNS manicures turning out fine and others requiring correction. Inconsistency like this often points to variation in how closely technicians follow the same application standards, rather than any single technician being consistently at fault. Standardizing key steps of the SNS process, and building in a final check before a client leaves the chair, can help close the gap between a salon's best work and its more uneven visits, so that quality does not depend heavily on which technician a client happens to get.

## A Cuticle Trim That Drew Blood

The client said that during her pedicure, the technician cut her big toe while trimming her cuticle, and the cut began bleeding during the service itself, with the toe still sore the following day. Cuticle trimming carries real risk when the cuticle is cut too close to the skin, and any nick that draws blood should be treated as a serious pause point rather than something to work around. Standard practice calls for immediately stopping, disinfecting the area, applying an antiseptic and bandage if needed, and letting the client know clearly what happened rather than continuing the service as though nothing occurred. Technicians should also be trained to trim conservatively around the cuticle line, erring on the side of leaving slightly more skin intact rather than risking a cut.

## A Color That Didn't Match the Swatch

On top of the injury, the client said the color she ended up with was purple, not the pink shown on the swatch she had chosen. A color mismatch on its own is a fixable error, but paired with an injury during the same service, it reinforced her sense that the visit had gone wrong in multiple ways. Double-checking the mixed or applied color against the client's chosen swatch before finishing the service is a quick habit that prevents this kind of avoidable discrepancy.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers several important takeaways. First, consistency in SNS application should be treated as a salon-wide standard, not something that varies by which technician a client happens to see, and periodic quality checks can help close that gap. Second, any cut or nick during cuticle work needs to be addressed immediately and transparently, with the service paused, the area properly treated, and the client informed, rather than continuing as if nothing happened. Third, color should always be checked against the client's chosen swatch before the service is considered finished. Addressing safety concerns directly and consistently, especially anything involving a cut or injury, is essential to keeping a client's trust intact.
